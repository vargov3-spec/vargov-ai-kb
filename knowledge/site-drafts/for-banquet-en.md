## Light in a banquet hall

**Subheading:** A room that changes its seating needs light that does not change with it.

A banquet hall lives in two states. In one it is empty and formal: guests come in, and what
works first is the volume — the height, the depth, what is visible from the doors. In the
other it is filled with tables, and the light is needed not in the volume but over the
cloth, the faces and the settings.

A composition usually answers for the first state, and that frees it from an impossible
task. It does not need to light the tables: the tables have their own group of light, set
out with the seating and changed along with it. The composition holds the volume — which is
why it can be large, sparse and warm rather than bright.

The practical consequence: a composition in a banquet hall is sized by the volume of the
room, not by the number of covers, and its position is not tied to the tables. The tables
will be moved.

### What to consider

**Two circuits of light, not one.**
The composition carries the volume and the occasion; the tables have a separate group that
changes with the seating. The total power of the composition is known before the order and
does not enter the calculation of light levels at the tables.

**The composition is not tied to the tables.**
Seating changes from one event to the next; the composition does not. Its centre is aligned
with the geometric centre of the room or with the axis of the entrance, not with the centre
of a table.

**The mounting plate goes in before the finishes.**
In banquet halls the slab is almost always concealed by a suspended ceiling, and the fixing
goes to the structural slab only — nothing fixes to the lining. A mounting plate rated for
the weight, and the layout of the suspension points, are needed before the ceiling goes up:
after the finishes this becomes an opening-up, not an installation. Weight and point count
are known in advance and are passed into the project.
